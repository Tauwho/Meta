# The intern screen now runs before anyone reads your résumé

*Many software internships now send a coding assessment before a human looks at you. Here is how to reorder your preparation.*

A student opens their résumé for the fifth time this month. They swap "built" for "developed", trim a bullet to fit one line, and move a project up the page. In another tab, an assessment link from the same company sits unopened. It expires in six days.

The résumé feels like the important document. It has their name on it. It is the thing career fairs and advice threads talk about. But for a growing number of software internships, it is not the first thing anyone checks.

The first gate is often a timed coding test that runs before a person reads a word. If that is true for the roles you want, the order you prepare in should change too: get ready for the test first, make the résumé good enough second, and polish last.

## The funnel got reordered

The old mental model of an internship application goes like this. You submit a résumé, a recruiter reads it, and if they like it you get a coding test or an interview.

For many large employers, the sequence now runs the other way. You submit an application, an automated online assessment arrives, often within days, and **a person reviews your résumé only if your score clears a threshold**. Early-career newsletters covering the Summer 2027 cycle describe exactly this pattern, with the assessment sitting in front of human review.

This is not universal. It is most common at companies that receive thousands of intern applications and need a cheap first filter. Smaller companies, startups and many local firms still read first, or go straight to a conversation. The point is not that every process looks like this. It is that enough of them do that your preparation should assume it.

The practical consequence is simple. A strong résumé attached to a failed assessment is, for that application, a résumé nobody reads.

## Why the résumé still matters, just later

It would be easy to overcorrect here and conclude that the résumé does not matter. That is wrong.

Once you pass the assessment, the résumé decides a lot. It shapes which interviews you get, which team looks at you, and what the interviewer asks about. It also still matters first at the companies that do not use automated screening.

So the claim is about sequencing, not abandonment. A résumé needs to be clear, correct and honest. It needs your projects described in terms of what they did, not adjectives. Beyond that, the returns from another round of rewording drop fast, and the hours would do more work elsewhere.

## What the assessment is testing now

The online assessment itself has changed as well. Reporting on the 2027 cycle describes companies redesigning intern assessments because junior applicants were using AI tools on them. The details vary, and much of the coverage is second-hand, so treat specifics as reported rather than confirmed.

The broad direction is consistent across sources, though. Assessments are more likely to be timed and monitored, with rules about AI use stated up front. Some add questions that ask you to explain or extend your solution, not only submit one. Separately, coverage of engineering interviews in 2026 describes a shift away from memorised algorithm drills towards reasoning about projects and showing how you work.

Two things follow for a student. First, being able to solve standard data structure problems under time pressure, without help, is still the price of entry. Second, the ability to explain why your solution works is becoming part of the test rather than something saved for the interview.

## A preparation order that fits the new funnel

If the test comes first, preparation should be ordered to match. This is the sequence I would suggest, with the trade-off stated plainly.

1. **Get assessment-ready on the core topics.** Arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, stacks and queues, trees, basic graphs. Practise under a timer, with no AI and no autocomplete, because that is how many assessments run.
2. **Make the résumé clear and correct, then stop.** One page, projects described by what they do and what you built, no typos, a link that works. Then close the file.
3. **Pick one or two projects you can explain in depth.** Know the design decisions, what broke, and what you would change. This serves both the newer assessment questions and the interview after.
4. **Apply in batches.** Assessments often arrive with short deadlines. Spacing your applications stops five tests landing in the same week.
5. **Review each assessment within a day.** Write down the problem types and where time went. This is the cheapest feedback you will get all cycle.

The cost of this order is real. Some applications will go out with a résumé that is good rather than excellent. If you are applying mainly to small companies that read every CV by hand, you may want to weight the résumé more heavily.

For most students applying to larger firms, though, the trade is worth it. An excellent résumé behind a failed test does nothing. A good résumé behind a passed test gets read.

## Before your next edit

None of this means the advice about résumés was wrong. It means it describes a step that now sits further down the funnel.

So before you open the document again, ask one question. Could you pass the test that decides whether anyone reads it?

If the answer is no, the next hour belongs to the test.
