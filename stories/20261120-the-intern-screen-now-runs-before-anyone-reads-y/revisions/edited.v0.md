# The intern screen now runs before anyone reads your résumé

*Many software internships now send a coding assessment before a human looks at you. Here is how to reorder your preparation.*

A student opens their résumé for the fifth time this month. They swap "built" for "developed", trim a bullet to fit one line, and move a project up the page. In another tab, an assessment link from the same company sits unopened. It expires in six days.

The résumé feels like the important document. It has their name on it. It is the thing career fairs and advice threads talk about. But for a growing number of software internships, it is not the first thing anyone checks.

The first gate is often a timed coding test that arrives before a person has read a word. If that is true for the roles you want, the order you prepare in should change too: get ready for the test first, make the résumé good enough second, and polish last.

## The funnel got reordered

The old mental model of an internship application goes like this. You submit a résumé, a recruiter reads it, and if they like it you get a coding test or an interview.

At many large employers, the order now looks different. You submit an application and an automated online assessment arrives, sometimes within minutes, often within days. Candidate reports describe these invitations going out [before anyone has reviewed the résumé](https://leonstaff.com/blogs/passed-coding-assessment-but-rejected/), and early-career newsletters covering the Summer 2027 cycle make the same argument: [tech tests you before it reads you](https://earlycareers.scalis.ai/p/tech-doesn-t-read-your-resume-first-it-tests-you).

What happens next is less clear from the outside. An instant invitation usually means only that your application [cleared some basic rules](https://prachub.com/resources/does-everyone-get-an-online-assessment-automatic-oas-resume-screens-and-what-the-invite-means). Your score is then weighed with everything else. But **a weak score can end the application before your résumé gets a proper look**, and that is the part worth planning around.

This is not universal. Some companies still screen the résumé first; several 2027 intern postings from ByteDance, for example, [list résumé screening before the assessment](https://prachub.com/resources/bytedance-swe-intern-oa-2027-codesignal-four-questions-and-resume-screening). Smaller firms and startups often go straight to a conversation. Assessment-first screening makes most sense for a company handling a large volume of applications, which is also where many students send most of theirs.

The practical consequence is simple. A strong résumé attached to a failed assessment may never be read closely.

## Why the résumé still matters, just later

It would be easy to overcorrect here and conclude that the résumé does not matter. That is wrong.

Once you pass the assessment, the résumé decides a lot. It shapes which interviews you get, which team looks at you, and what the interviewer asks about. It also still matters first at the companies that read before they test.

So the claim is about **sequencing, not abandonment**. A résumé needs to be clear, correct and honest, with projects described by what they did rather than by adjectives. Beyond that, the returns from another round of rewording drop fast, and the hours would do more work elsewhere.

A useful test: if you cannot name what a new edit changes for the reader, it is probably not worth the time this week.

## What the assessment is testing now

The assessment itself has changed as well. Reporting on the 2027 cycle describes companies [rebuilding the intern screen](https://earlycareers.scalis.ai/p/the-intern-screen-got-rebuilt-juniors-are-the-target) because junior applicants were using AI tools on it. Much of that coverage is second-hand, so treat the details as reported rather than confirmed.

The direction is consistent, though. Described changes include locked-down test environments, camera and screen proctoring, work-sample tasks, and follow-up questions that ask you to explain or extend your solution. Separately, coverage of engineering interviews in 2026 describes a [shift away from memorised algorithm drills](https://letsdatascience.com/news/ai-changes-software-engineering-hiring-practices-da41ee53) towards reasoning about projects and showing how you work.

Two things follow. First, solving standard data structure problems under time pressure is still the price of entry. Second, **explaining why your solution works is becoming part of the test**, not something saved for the interview.

## A preparation order that fits the new funnel

If the test can come first, preparation should be ordered to match. This is the sequence I would suggest, with the trade-off stated plainly.

1. **Get assessment-ready on the core topics.** Arrays, strings, hash maps, two pointers, stacks and queues, trees, basic graphs. Practise under a timer and without AI help, because many assessments ban or monitor it.
2. **Make the résumé clear and correct, then stop.** One page, projects described by what they do and what you built, no typos, links that work. Then close the file.
3. **Pick one or two projects you can explain in depth.** Know the design decisions, what broke, and what you would change. This serves the newer follow-up questions and the interview after.
4. **Apply in batches.** Assessments often come with a fixed window to complete them. Spacing your applications stops five tests landing in the same week.
5. **Review each assessment within a day.** Write down the problem types and where your time went. This is the cheapest feedback you will get all cycle.

The cost of this order is real. Some applications will go out with a résumé that is good rather than excellent. If you are applying mainly to small companies that read every CV by hand, weight the résumé more heavily.

For students applying to larger firms, though, the trade is worth it. A good résumé behind a passed test gets read. An excellent one behind a failed test may not.

## Before your next edit

None of this means the advice about résumés was wrong. It describes a step that now often sits further down the funnel.

So before you open the document again, ask one thing. Could you pass the test that decides how closely anyone reads it?

If the answer is no, the next hour belongs to the test.
